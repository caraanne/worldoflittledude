Why Won't My 4-Year-Old Listen? 7 Things to Try Instead of Yelling

Practical ways to help children ages 3–7 listen, cooperate, and follow directions without constant reminders.

"Put your shoes on, please." Nothing. "Can you put your shoes on?" Still nothing. "SHOES. NOW!" And suddenly, you're the one shouting, your child is upset, and you're both having a terrible morning before you've even left the house.

Sound familiar?

As an early childhood educator and Montessori teacher, I've spent years helping young children develop listening skills, independence, and cooperation. And one thing I've learned is that children don't automatically know how to listen and follow directions. These are skills we need to teach and practise.

The good news? You don't need to spend your entire day repeating yourself.

1. Why Won't My 4-Year-Old Listen?

If your 4-year-old isn't listening, you're certainly not alone. At this age, children are still developing the attention, memory, impulse control, and communication skills needed to follow instructions consistently.

Common reasons children struggle to listen include:

  • They're distracted. Young children can become deeply focused on play.
  • The instruction is too complicated. Multiple directions can overwhelm working memory.
  • They're tired, hungry, or overstimulated. Cooperation becomes harder when basic needs aren't met.
  • They don't want to stop playing. Transitions are particularly challenging at this age.
  • They're testing their independence. Children are discovering that they have their own opinions and preferences.
  • They need more time to process. Some children take longer to understand and respond to verbal instructions.

A child who doesn't follow directions isn't necessarily being deliberately defiant. Before assuming your child is ignoring you, consider whether they understand what's being asked and have the skills and support to follow through.

2. Listening Is a Skill, Not Just a Behaviour

Think about everything involved in following a simple instruction like "Put your shoes away." Your child needs to stop what they're doing, shift their attention to you, understand your words, remember the instruction, and then take action. That's quite a lot for a developing brain!

In Montessori education, we place enormous value on modelling, repetition, and giving children opportunities to practise independence. Rather than constantly telling children what to do, we demonstrate routines and create environments that help them succeed.

For example, if you want your child to put their shoes away, having a clearly designated shoe basket near the door makes the task easier. You can show them where the shoes belong, practise putting them away together, and gradually encourage them to do it independently.

The goal isn't simply to get children to obey. It's to help them develop the skills to cooperate, take responsibility, and manage everyday routines.

3. Seven Ways to Help Your 4-Year-Old Listen

1. Get Their Attention Before Giving Instructions

Instead of calling instructions from another room, walk over to your child. Say their name, get down to their level, and wait until you've got their attention. You might gently say: "Charlie, I have something to tell you." Then give your instruction. The important thing is making sure you've connected before expecting a response.

2. Keep Instructions Short and Simple

"Go upstairs, put your pajamas on, brush your teeth, put your clothes in the laundry basket, and choose a book." That's a lot of information! Young children often manage better with one or two directions at a time. Try: "First, put on your pajamas." Once that's done: "Now let's brush your teeth."

3. Show Them What You Mean

Children learn by watching and doing. If you're constantly saying "Clean up your toys," try demonstrating what cleaning up actually looks like. Pick up a few blocks and place them in the basket. "Watch. The blocks go in here." Then invite your child to help. Show first. Practise together. Then encourage independence.

4. Offer Two Acceptable Choices

Children ages 3–7 are developing a strong sense of independence. Offering choices can help them feel involved without giving up necessary boundaries. Instead of "Put your coat on right now!" try "Would you like to put on your coat by yourself, or would you like some help?" Both choices move the routine forward.

5. Turn Everyday Instructions Into Games

Instead of repeating "Put your toys away" five times, try: "Can you find all the red blocks and put them in the basket?" Or: "Let's see if we can tidy up before the song finishes!" You can also experiment with silly voices, animal movements, or pretending to be robots. "Robot mission: Find your shoes!" Playfulness can turn an everyday demand into an opportunity for connection.

6. Give Children Time to Respond

Adults often expect children to respond immediately. But young children may need extra time to process an instruction or transition away from an activity. After giving a clear direction, pause. Resist the urge to repeat yourself straightaway. If your child still doesn't respond, check whether they understood: "What do we need to do next?"

7. Notice Cooperation When It Happens

We often spend so much time correcting behaviour that we forget to notice when children are cooperating. Try acknowledging specific actions: "You put your shoes away without another reminder." "You stopped playing when it was time for dinner." Rather than constant rewards or exaggerated praise, focus on genuine encouragement.

4. What to Do When Your Child Still Refuses to Listen

Even with the best strategies, there will be moments when your child simply says "No!" First, consider whether your request is reasonable for their age. Then stay calm and hold the boundary.

"I know you want to keep playing. It's time to leave." You can acknowledge their disappointment without changing the expectation. If they're struggling with a task, offer support: "Would you like to start, or shall we do the first part together?"

Avoid turning every disagreement into a power struggle. Sometimes the most effective response is fewer words, a clear expectation, and a little help getting started.

5. Create Routines That Reduce Repeating Yourself

One of the most useful things we can do for young children is create predictable routines. When children know what happens next, they don't need as many verbal reminders.

A simple morning routine might be: get dressed, eat breakfast, brush teeth, put on shoes, collect backpack. You could use photographs or drawings to create a visual routine chart. Instead of repeatedly saying "What are you supposed to be doing?" you can point to the chart and ask: "What's next?"

Over time, your child can begin taking more responsibility for their own routine, building independence and executive functioning skills while reducing daily reminders.

6. Practise Listening Skills Through Play

Some of the best opportunities to develop listening skills happen through games.

Simon Says

A classic game that helps children practise listening carefully, remembering instructions, and controlling impulses. Start with simple actions such as touching your nose or jumping twice.

Freeze Dance

Play music and invite your child to dance. When the music stops, everyone freezes! This playful activity helps children practise attention, stopping, and starting.

The Silly Instructions Game

Give your child funny directions: "Touch your head and moo like a cow!" "Hop three times and make a silly face!" Then let your child become the leader and give you instructions. These games give children opportunities to practise listening without the pressure of everyday tasks.

7. When Should You Be Concerned About Listening Difficulties?

It's normal for young children to need reminders and support. However, if your child consistently struggles to understand simple directions, rarely responds when spoken to, or experiences significant difficulties at home and school, consider speaking with your pediatrician.

Hearing differences, language development, attention difficulties, and other developmental factors can affect how children respond to instructions. A pediatrician can help determine whether additional assessment or support would be useful.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a 4-year-old not to listen?

Yes. Four-year-olds are still developing attention, impulse control, working memory, and the ability to follow directions. Occasional resistance and the need for reminders are common. Persistent difficulties understanding or responding to instructions may warrant further guidance.

How do I get my 4-year-old to listen without yelling?

Get close before speaking, use short instructions, offer limited choices, and create predictable routines. Model what you want your child to do and give them time to respond. Staying calm and consistent often works better than raising your voice.

How many instructions should a 4-year-old be able to follow?

Many four-year-olds can follow familiar two- or three-step directions, particularly when they're calm and paying attention. Complex or unfamiliar instructions may need to be broken into smaller steps.

Why does my child listen to their teacher but not to me?

Classrooms often have predictable routines, visual reminders, and consistent expectations. At home, tiredness, emotional needs, and different routines can affect cooperation. It doesn't mean your child respects one adult more than another.

Less repeating, more connecting.

There will always be mornings when getting out the door feels like an Olympic event. But listening and cooperation aren't skills children develop overnight. They grow through modelling, practice, consistency, and connection.

Instead of asking "How can I make my child listen?" try asking "How can I make it easier for my child to understand, remember, and follow through?" It's a small shift that can make everyday parenting feel a little less exhausting.
