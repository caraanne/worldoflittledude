What to Do When Kids Won't Listen

Practical strategies for the moments when you've said it three times and they're still not hearing you.

You've said it three times. You've said it louder. You've said it in your "I mean it" voice. And they're still just... not listening. Sound familiar? You're not alone, and more importantly - you're not doing anything wrong.

Why kids "don't listen" (it's not what you think)

Here's the thing: most of the time when kids don't listen, it's not defiance. It's development. The part of the brain responsible for following instructions, switching tasks and impulse control - the prefrontal cortex - isn't fully developed until the mid-twenties. Yes, really. Which means your 4-year-old is doing their absolute best with a brain that is literally not finished yet.

That doesn't mean we accept the behaviour. It means we understand it - and then we deal with it smarter.

Get down to their level (literally)

Before you repeat yourself a fourth time, try this: crouch down, make eye contact, and use their name. Not "HEY!" - their actual name. "Oscar. I need you to look at me." Physical proximity and eye contact tell the brain that something important is happening. It bypasses the noise and gets through.

Give them one instruction at a time

Adults can process a string of instructions. Kids aged 3–7 really can't. "Get your shoes, put your bag by the door, wash your hands and come for breakfast" is four instructions. Pick one. "Can you please get your shoes?" Done. Then the next one. It feels slower. It's actually faster.

Give a warning before a transition

Most "not listening" happens at transition points - when you're asking them to stop something they're enjoying. A two-minute warning changes everything. "In two minutes we're leaving the park." It gives their brain time to prepare, instead of being asked to switch gears out of nowhere.

Try connection before correction

If a child is mid-meltdown, mid-play or mid-anything, their nervous system is occupied. You won't get through with instructions until they feel connected to you first. A hand on the shoulder, "I can see you're really into this" - it takes 20 seconds and makes the rest go so much smoother.

Check: are they actually able to do what you're asking?

Sometimes kids don't listen because they genuinely don't know how to do what we're asking. "Be kind to your sister" is not an instruction - it's a destination. "Can you use a gentle voice when you talk to her?" is something they can actually do.
