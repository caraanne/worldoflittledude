How to Raise a Confident Child

Confidence is built in the small everyday moments - here's how to make the most of them.

Confidence in children isn't something you can give them - but it is something you can build, slowly and steadily, through the everyday moments that probably don't feel significant at all.

Confidence comes from doing hard things

The fastest way to build a child's confidence is to let them struggle a little. Not suffer - struggle. When we rush in to fix everything before they've had a chance to try, we accidentally send the message: "I don't think you can do this." When we wait, when we say "I know this is hard, keep going" - we send a completely different one.

Praise the effort, not the result

You've probably heard this before, but it genuinely matters. "You worked so hard on that" lands differently to "You're so smart." Praising effort tells children that trying is what counts - and that means they'll keep trying even when things are difficult. Praising talent tells them their ability is fixed, and they'll avoid challenges that might prove otherwise.

Let them make choices

Confidence grows when children feel a sense of agency - that their choices matter and that they have some control over their world. You don't need to give them unlimited choices. "Would you like to wear the red shirt or the blue one?" is more than enough. Small choices add up to a big sense of self.

Teach them to try again

Children who believe they can get better at things - what researchers call a growth mindset - are more confident, more resilient and more willing to take on challenges. The simplest way to build this? Normalise "not yet." "You can't do it yet. Let's keep practising."

Notice the small brave things

Confidence isn't just about big moments. It's built in the small ones - the time they spoke up when they didn't want to, the time they tried a new food, the time they kept going when something felt hard. Notice those. Name them. "That was brave. I saw that."

Model it yourself

Children learn confidence by watching the adults around them handle uncertainty, mistakes and hard things. When you say "I'm not sure, but let's figure it out together" or "I got that wrong - I'll try again," you're teaching confidence more powerfully than any lesson ever could.
