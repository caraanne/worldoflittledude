Why Won't My Child Share? 7 Ways to Teach Sharing Without Forcing It

Practical ways to help children ages 3–7 learn to share, take turns, and play cooperatively.

You're at the playground. Your child is happily playing with a toy when another child approaches.

"Can I have a turn?"

Your child clutches the toy tightly.

"NO! IT'S MINE!"

You feel the other parent's eyes on you. You smile awkwardly, encourage your child to share, and suddenly you're negotiating over a plastic truck as though it's the most valuable object in the world.

Sound familiar?

As an early childhood educator and Montessori teacher, I've seen this scenario play out countless times. And here's something I always remind parents: sharing is a skill children learn through practice, not something they're automatically born knowing how to do.

The good news? There are simple ways to help children become more generous, patient, and cooperative without forcing them to hand over everything they love.

1. Why Won't My Child Share?

For young children, sharing can be surprisingly complicated.

Imagine you're enjoying your morning coffee when someone walks over, takes it out of your hands, and tells you it's their turn. You probably wouldn't be thrilled either!

Young children are still developing an understanding of ownership, fairness, patience, and other people's perspectives. Between ages 3 and 7, children gradually become better at negotiating, waiting, and cooperating with others. But these skills don't develop overnight.

Common reasons children struggle to share include:

  • A strong sense of ownership. They're beginning to understand what belongs to them.
  • Difficulty waiting. Delaying gratification is still challenging.
  • Limited perspective-taking. Understanding what someone else wants or feels takes practice.
  • Fear of losing something. They may worry that a favourite toy won't be returned.
  • Being deeply engaged in play. They aren't necessarily ready to stop what they're doing.
  • Feeling tired or overwhelmed. Sharing becomes harder when emotions are running high.

A child who refuses to share isn't automatically selfish or unkind. Often, they're simply learning how to navigate the complicated world of relationships.

2. Sharing and Taking Turns Aren't Exactly the Same Thing

We often use the words sharing and taking turns interchangeably, but they're slightly different skills.

Sharing can mean allowing someone else to use something, offering part of what we have, or enjoying something together. Taking turns means waiting while someone else has a chance to use an item or participate in an activity.

For young children, taking turns is often easier to understand because it has a clearer beginning and end.

Rather than saying: "You need to share your truck!" try: "You're using the truck right now. When you're finished, Sam can have a turn." Or: "Would you like to build the track together?"

These approaches help children understand that cooperation doesn't always mean immediately giving up what they're enjoying. And that's an important lesson.

3. Seven Simple Ways to Teach Children to Share

The best way to teach sharing is through everyday experiences, modelling, and play. Here are seven practical strategies you can try at home.

1. Practise Taking Turns With Games

Start with simple games that naturally involve waiting. Rolling a ball back and forth, taking turns adding blocks to a tower, or playing a basic board game are all great options. Use clear language: "My turn." "Your turn." "Now it's your turn again!"

For younger children, keep waiting periods short. As they become more comfortable, gradually introduce longer turns. The goal is to help children experience waiting and turn-taking in a relaxed, enjoyable setting.

2. Use a Timer When It Helps

Timers can make taking turns feel more predictable. If two children both want the same toy, you might say: "Would you like to use the timer so you both know when it's time to switch?" Let the children help choose a reasonable amount of time. When the timer finishes, calmly support the transition.

However, timers aren't always necessary. If a child is deeply engaged in meaningful play, automatically interrupting them every few minutes can create unnecessary frustration. Use timers as a tool, not a rule for every situation.

3. Model Sharing in Everyday Life

Children learn an enormous amount by watching adults. Look for natural opportunities to demonstrate generosity and cooperation. You might say: "I have some strawberries. Would you like me to share them with you?" Or: "There's one big blanket. Let's see if we can both fit underneath!"

And remember to model respectful boundaries too. It's okay to say: "I'm using this right now. You can have it when I'm finished." Sharing works best when children learn to respect both generosity and ownership.

4. Prepare for Playdates

Before friends arrive, talk about which toys your child feels comfortable sharing. You might say: "Your friend is coming over. Which toys would you like to play with together?" If your child has a particularly special toy, it's perfectly reasonable to put it away before the playdate. This helps prevent unnecessary conflict.

Then set out toys that encourage cooperative play, such as blocks, train tracks, pretend food, or art materials. Preparing the environment is a simple Montessori-inspired strategy that can make social interactions easier.

5. Teach Children What to Say

Sometimes children grab toys because they don't yet know how to ask for a turn. Practise helpful phrases together:

  • "Can I have a turn when you're finished?"
  • "Can we play together?"
  • "I'm still using this."
  • "You can have it next."
  • "Let's build something together."

Role-play with stuffed animals or toy figures. Pretend Bunny wants Teddy's truck. What could Bunny say? What could Teddy say? Keep the activity playful and let your child suggest solutions.

Practising these conversations when everyone is calm makes the words easier to remember during real disagreements.

6. Notice Acts of Kindness

When your child shares spontaneously, acknowledge what happened. "You offered your crayons to your friend." "You noticed that your sister wanted a turn." "That was thoughtful of you."

Rather than offering a reward every time your child shares, help them notice the positive impact of their actions. You might ask: "How did your friend feel when you invited her to play?" These conversations encourage children to think about other people's experiences.

7. Don't Force Sharing

This might be the most important tip of all. Imagine being told you must hand over something you're using simply because someone else wants it. Forced sharing can create resentment rather than generosity.

Instead, acknowledge both children's needs. "You're still playing with the truck, and Maya would like a turn too." Then help them find a solution. "Can Maya have a turn when you're finished?" "Is there another truck she could use?" "Would you like to build a road together?"

There will still be situations where adults need to establish fair rules, particularly with shared classroom materials or limited resources. But whenever possible, involve children in finding solutions rather than simply taking the toy away.

We want children to understand why sharing matters, not just learn to hand something over when an adult tells them to.

4. What to Say When Your Child Refuses to Share

It's easy to feel embarrassed when your child shouts "MINE!" in front of other parents. But these moments are opportunities to teach.

  • Instead of "Stop being selfish!" Try: "You're enjoying that toy. Let's work out how everyone can have a turn."
  • Instead of "Give it to her right now!" Try: "She's waiting for a turn. What could we do?"
  • Instead of "Good children share!" Try: "Sharing and taking turns help us play together."

Avoid labelling your child as selfish, greedy, or naughty. Focus on the specific skill they're learning. And remember, your child doesn't have to surrender every personal belonging simply because another child asks for it. Learning to say "I'm still using this" politely is also an important social skill.

5. What If Your Child Grabs Toys From Other Children?

Grabbing is common in early childhood, particularly when children are excited or frustrated. If your child grabs a toy, calmly intervene.

"I won't let you take that from her hands." Help return the toy if appropriate, then acknowledge your child's desire. "You really wanted that truck." Now model an alternative. "Let's ask, 'Can I have a turn when you're finished?'"

If your child is too upset to practise the words, help them settle first. Later, when everyone is calm, you can role-play the situation with toys. The goal isn't to shame children for making mistakes. It's to give them the tools to handle similar situations differently next time.

6. Three Fun Sharing Games to Try at Home

Children learn best when they're actively involved. Here are three simple activities that help build sharing and turn-taking skills.

The Pass-the-Present Game

Wrap a small toy or object in several layers of paper. Play music while children pass the parcel around. When the music stops, the child holding it removes one layer. Keep going until the final surprise is revealed. For younger children, you can adapt the game so everyone gets a chance to unwrap a layer.

Build Together Challenge

Give two children a pile of blocks and one challenge: "Can you build the tallest tower together?" Encourage them to take turns adding blocks, share ideas, and work as a team. Afterwards, ask: "What helped you build together?"

The Sharing Picnic

Set up a pretend picnic using toy food, plates, and stuffed animals. Invite your child to serve everyone. "Teddy would like some apples. Can you give him some?" "Bunny needs a plate too!" This playful activity encourages children to think about other people's needs while practising generosity and cooperation.

7. When Should You Be Concerned About Sharing Difficulties?

It's normal for young children to find sharing difficult, especially with favourite toys or in unfamiliar situations. However, if your child consistently struggles with peer interactions, becomes extremely distressed during everyday turn-taking, or frequently responds with aggression, it may be helpful to speak with their teacher or pediatrician.

Sometimes children need additional support with communication, emotional regulation, or social understanding. The aim isn't to force children to become generous overnight. It's to understand what skills they need help developing.

Frequently Asked Questions About Teaching Children to Share

Is it normal for a 3-year-old not to share?

Yes. Three-year-olds are still developing patience, perspective-taking, and an understanding of ownership. Taking turns can be easier than sharing because it provides a predictable structure. Frequent modelling and playful practice can help.

Should I force my child to share their toys?

Generally, forcing children to immediately give up personal belongings isn't the most helpful way to teach sharing. Instead, model turn-taking, encourage cooperation, and help children understand other people's needs. Shared materials may require clear, consistent rules.

At what age do children learn to share?

Sharing develops gradually throughout early childhood. Many children begin showing more cooperative sharing and turn-taking skills during the preschool years, but individual development varies. Children continue learning negotiation, patience, and fairness well into the school years.

How do I teach my child to share without getting upset?

Start by practising turn-taking through games when your child is calm. Use simple language, model respectful requests, and prepare for situations where sharing may be difficult. Acknowledge frustration while maintaining fair boundaries.

Sharing Is About More Than Toys

When we teach children to share, we're helping them develop much more than the ability to hand over a toy. We're teaching patience, communication, empathy, cooperation, and respect for others. And these are skills that will support them throughout their lives.

There will still be arguments over trucks, dolls, and whose turn it is to press the elevator button. That's all part of growing up.

The goal isn't to raise a child who always gives everything away. It's to raise a child who understands how to be generous while also respecting their own needs and boundaries.
